Important Things to Know

Before consenting to whole genome sequencing, it is important you understand these key considerations. Please read each section carefully.

⚠️

Unexpected Findings

⚠️
You may learn things about your health that you were not expecting

Whole genome sequencing looks at all of your DNA. This means it may reveal health information that is unrelated to the original reason for your test.

Because WGS reads your entire genome, it can sometimes identify genetic changes that have nothing to do with why the test was requested. These are known as secondary or incidental findings.

Examples of unexpected findings might include:

  • A previously undiagnosed condition — for example, familial hypercholesterolaemia (a genetic condition affecting cholesterol levels). Such findings can have medical implications for you and your family, and may also affect financial matters such as life insurance
  • Discovery that you are a carrier for a genetic condition (meaning you could pass it to your children, even if you're unaffected yourself)
  • An increased risk of developing a condition in the future, such as a predisposition to certain cancers
  • A pharmacogenomic variant — a genetic change that affects how your body processes certain medications

If unexpected findings are discovered, you may be referred for further tests or investigations to understand what they mean for you. This is covered in Point 3 of the Record of Discussion form.

Non-Paternity / Misattributed Parentage

🔴
Genomic testing can reveal that biological relationships within a family are different from those assumed

This is a sensitive but important consideration. If discovered, this information must be disclosed under current UK guidance.

Whole genome sequencing analyses your DNA in such detail that it can determine biological relationships between individuals. This is especially relevant for trio testing — where the patient and both biological parents are sequenced together (see Trio Testing).

How Trio Testing Reveals Non-Paternity

In trio testing, the patient's genome is compared directly with both parents. Because a child inherits approximately half their DNA from each biological parent, the analysis will clearly show if a stated parent is not biologically related. This means WGS can reveal:

  • That a person's biological father is not who they believed (non-paternity)
  • That a person was adopted or conceived using donor gametes
  • That siblings are half-siblings rather than full siblings
  • Other unexpected family relationships

Even in duo testing (patient + one parent) or singleton testing (patient only), unexpected family relationships can sometimes come to light through variant analysis and comparison with other family members or population databases.

Why This Must Be Disclosed

Under current UK guidance, if genomic testing reveals misattributed parentage, the clinical team has a duty to share this information because:

  • Clinical accuracy: If a stated parent is not the biological parent, the trio analysis is based on incorrect assumptions. This directly affects the ability to identify disease-causing variants and could lead to a missed or incorrect diagnosis
  • Family health implications: Accurate biological relationships are critical for genetic counselling, carrier testing, and risk assessment for all family members
  • Recurrence risk: Understanding true parentage is essential for assessing the risk of the condition occurring in future children
  • Duty of candour: The NHS has a duty of honesty and transparency with patients about findings from their tests

This will always be handled sensitively and confidentially. You will be offered support, including referral to genetic counselling services, to help you process this information.

👨‍👩‍👧
Before Agreeing to Trio Testing

Both parents providing samples for trio testing should be aware before consenting that the analysis will confirm or rule out biological parentage. This should be discussed openly with your clinical team or genetic counsellor before samples are taken. If there is any concern about non-paternity, this can be discussed privately and confidentially with your healthcare professional.

Uncertain Results (Variants of Uncertain Significance)

🔍
Not all genetic changes can be clearly classified as harmful or harmless

A Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) is a genetic change whose impact on health is not yet fully understood.

Genetic science is still evolving. When your genome is sequenced, scientists may find changes in your DNA that haven't been seen before, or that haven't been studied enough to know whether they cause health problems.

Key things to understand about VUS results:

  • A VUS is not a diagnosis — it means more information is needed
  • Your results may be re-analysed in the future as scientific knowledge improves. A VUS today could be reclassified as either harmful or harmless later
  • You may be asked to return for follow-up appointments or further testing
  • Living with uncertainty can be psychologically challenging — support is available through genetic counselling services

This is covered in Point 2 of the Record of Discussion form (Uncertainty) and is an important aspect of WGS to understand before consenting.

Insurance Implications

✅
Good news: You generally do NOT need to disclose predictive genetic test results to insurers

The Association of British Insurers (ABI) Code on Genetic Testing and Insurance protects patients.

Many patients worry about whether genomic test results will affect their insurance. Here are the key facts:

  • Predictive results: You do not usually have to tell an insurer about predictive genetic test results — that is, results showing you might develop a condition in the future
  • Diagnosed conditions: If a genetic test confirms a diagnosis of a condition you already have, you must disclose the condition itself (as with any other medical diagnosis), but not the genetic test specifically
  • No requirement to test: No insurer can require or pressure you to take a genetic test
  • No access to raw data: Insurers do not have the right to ask for your raw genomic data
  • NHS protection: The NHS will not give your genetic test results to an insurance company without your explicit consent

This protection is governed by the ABI Code on Genetic Testing and Insurance, a voluntary agreement between the UK government and the insurance industry.

Data Storage & Sharing

ℹ️
Your genomic data is stored nationally and cannot be opted out of

Even if you have opted out of NHS data sharing, this does not apply to genomic test data.

Key facts about how your genomic data is handled:

  • Your DNA sample (blood, saliva, or tissue) will be stored by the NHS laboratory even after testing is complete
  • Data from WGS is stored in a secure national database
  • Where necessary, identifying information (name, address) is removed or separated from the genomic data
  • Your data can only be accessed by approved staff
  • Your data may be used for: your individual care, planning NHS services, and research
  • It is not possible to stop your genomic data being stored and shared — this is a necessary part of the NHS Genomic Medicine Service

This is covered in Points 4, 5, and 6 of the Record of Discussion form.

More information: NHS England Privacy Notice

Research Participation

🔬
You will be asked whether you want to contribute to the National Genomic Research Library

This is entirely voluntary. Saying no will not affect your clinical care.

The National Genomic Research Library, managed by Genomics England, combines genomic data from many patients to help researchers better understand disease and develop new treatments.

If you agree to participate:

  • Genomics England will be able to access your personal data including your genomic record
  • Your data will be stored securely and steps taken to ensure you cannot be personally identified
  • Your clinical team or Genomics England can contact you if research reveals something relevant to your health
  • Researchers may include national/international scientists, healthcare companies, and NHS staff — all approved by an independent committee
  • No personal insurers or marketing companies will have access
  • You can withdraw at any time

This is covered in full on Page 2 of the Record of Discussion form.
