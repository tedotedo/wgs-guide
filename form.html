Your Privacy — No Data Is Collected

This website does not collect, store, or transmit any of your personal data. All information you enter is kept only in your browser (localStorage) and is never sent to any server. When you are finished, you can download a PDF to email to your consultant. You may clear your browser data at any time to remove all saved information.

Are You Completing Forms for a Trio Test?

If your child is being tested, the test works best when both parents also provide a DNA sample (called a trio test). This means you may need to complete two forms:
1. One for yourself (as a participant in the trio — select "I am the patient")
2. One on behalf of your child (select "I am a parent / guardian")
Complete your first form, download the PDF, then use the "Start a New Form" button at the bottom to begin the second one.

I have discussed genomic testing with my health professional and understand the following. Please read each point and tick to confirm you understand. Click "Explain this to me" for a plain-English explanation.

All of the statements below remain relevant even if the test relates to someone other than yourself, for example your child.

1
Family and wider implications

The results of my test may have implications for me and members of my family. I understand that my results may also be used to help the healthcare of members of my family and others nationally and internationally. This could be done in discussion with me or through a process that will not personally identify me.

What this means: Your genetic results don't just affect you — they can provide important health information for your blood relatives too. For example, if a genetic variant is found that causes a condition, your siblings or children may also carry it.

Your anonymised data may also be compared with other patients' data nationally and internationally to help scientists better understand genetic conditions. This comparison helps determine whether a genetic change is significant or common and harmless.

If comparison involves discussion about specific results, this will happen with you directly. If it's done through databases, you won't be personally identified.

Learn more about family implications →

2
Uncertainty

The results of my test may have findings that are uncertain and not yet fully understood. To decide whether findings are significant for myself or others, my data may be compared to other patients' results across the country and internationally. I understand that this could change what my results mean for me and my treatment over time.

What this means: Genomic science is still evolving. Your test may find changes in your DNA called Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS) — changes whose impact on health isn't yet fully understood.

As more people have WGS and more research is done, the significance of these variants may become clearer. This means your results could potentially be re-interpreted in the future, which might change your diagnosis or treatment.

Learn more about uncertain results →

3
Unexpected information

The results of my test may also reveal unexpected results that are not related to why I am having this test. These may be found by chance and I may need further tests or investigations to understand their significance.

What this means: Because WGS reads all of your DNA, it may discover genetic changes unrelated to the original reason for your test. For example, it might find you carry a gene associated with a different health condition.

These are sometimes called "incidental" or "secondary" findings. If something significant is found, you may need additional tests or specialist referrals to understand what it means for your health.

This also includes the possibility of discovering unexpected family relationships (such as non-paternity), particularly when trio testing is performed. Read more about this →

Learn more about unexpected findings →

4
DNA storage

Normal NHS laboratory practice is to store the DNA extracted from my sample even after my current testing is complete. My DNA might be used for future analysis and/or to ensure that other testing (for example that of family members) is of high quality.

What this means: After your test is complete, the NHS laboratory will keep the DNA they extracted from your sample. This is standard practice and serves two purposes:

  • Future re-analysis: As genomic science advances, your stored DNA can be re-analysed with updated knowledge, potentially leading to new findings without needing a new sample.
  • Quality assurance: When family members are tested later, your stored DNA can be used to verify results and ensure accuracy.

Learn more about data storage →

5
Data storage

The data from my genomic test will be securely stored so that it can be looked at again in the future if necessary.

What this means: Your genomic data (the digital results from sequencing your DNA) will be stored in a secure national database. This is separate from your physical DNA sample.

Key points:

  • Data is stored securely with strict access controls
  • Only approved staff can access your data
  • Where possible, identifying information is separated from the genomic data
  • Important: You cannot opt out of having your genomic data stored, even if you have an NHS data opt-out in place

Learn more about data storage →

6
Health records

Results from my genomic test will be part of my patient record, a copy of which is held in a national system only available to healthcare professionals.

What this means: Just like any other medical test, the results of your genomic test will become part of your medical record. This means:

  • Your clinical team (doctors, nurses, genetic counsellors) can access your results to inform your care
  • A copy is held on the National Genomic Medicine Service database, accessible only to authorised healthcare professionals
  • This helps ensure continuity of care if you see different specialists or move to a different area
7
Research

I understand that I have the opportunity to take part in research which may benefit myself or others, now or in the future. An offer to join a national research opportunity is available on the following page.

What this means: On the next page of this form, you'll be invited to consider contributing your data to the National Genomic Research Library, managed by Genomics England.

This is entirely voluntary — you can say yes or no, and it will not affect your clinical care or test results. If you're unsure, you can discuss this further with your healthcare professional.

Learn more about research participation →

Step 1 of 3 — Discussion Points

🔬 The National Genomic Research Library

ℹ️

The NHS invites you to contribute to the National Genomic Research Library, managed by Genomics England. Participation is voluntary and will not affect your clinical care. Please read the information below and then make your choice.

Genomics England was set up in 2013 by the Department of Health and Social Care to work with the NHS to build a library of human genomes for researchers to study. Combining data from many different patients helps researchers to better understand disease and spot patterns in the data.

By agreeing to share your data you might get results which could lead to your own diagnosis, a new treatment, or offers to take part in clinical trials. Your taking part could enable diagnoses for people who don't have one.

By saying 'yes' to research, I understand the following:

NHS England, on behalf of the Trusts that provided your genomic test, will allow Genomics England to access my personal data including my genomic record.

What this means: Genomics England will be able to see your test results and relevant medical information so they can include it in their research database.

Any samples and data stored by Genomics England and the NHS will always be stored securely. Genomics England will take all reasonable steps to ensure that I cannot be personally identified.

My clinical team or Genomics England together with my clinical team, can contact me if the data or samples reveals any clinical trials or other research that I might benefit from.

If something is relevant to me or my family, there is a process by which this will be shared with my NHS clinical team.

Researchers may include national or international scientists, healthcare companies and NHS staff. To access the data, these researchers must all be approved by an independent committee of experts, including health professionals, clinical academics and patients. There will be no access to the data by personal insurers and marketing companies.

Genomics England will collect different aspects of my health data from the NHS and other data from organisations listed at genomicsengland.co.uk/privacy-policy. The collection and analysis of my health data for research will continue across my entire lifetime and beyond.

I can change my mind about taking part at any time.

More information regarding research in the National Genomic Research Library can be found at www.genomicsengland.co.uk. For any further questions, my healthcare professional can provide information.
