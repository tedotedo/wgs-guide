Understanding Whole Genome Sequencing

Everything you need to know about WGS — what it is, how it works, what results can show, and how it may affect you and your family.

🧬 What Is a Genome?

Your genome is the complete set of instructions that make you who you are. It's made up of DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) — a long molecule found in almost every cell of your body.

Your DNA is organised into 23 pairs of chromosomes, and within these are around 20,000 genes. Genes are sections of DNA that contain the instructions for making proteins, which carry out most of the work in your cells.

📊
Key fact: Your genome contains approximately 3 billion pairs of DNA "letters" (called base pairs). If you printed it out, it would fill about 200 volumes the size of a telephone book.

🔬 What Is Whole Genome Sequencing?

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is a type of genetic test that reads all of your DNA at once — your entire genome. This is different from older genetic tests that only look at specific genes or small sections of your DNA.

By analysing all 3 billion base pairs, WGS can identify changes (called variants) that may be causing a health condition or increasing your risk of developing one.

💡
How it differs: Traditional genetic tests are like searching a specific chapter of a book for a typo. WGS reads the entire book, making it much more comprehensive.

🏥 Why Has WGS Been Recommended?

WGS is offered through the NHS Genomic Medicine Service for specific clinical indications. You may have been referred because:

  • You or your child has a rare or inherited disease that hasn't been diagnosed by other tests
  • You have a cancer that may benefit from genomic analysis to guide treatment (cancer WGS analyses both the tumour and normal tissue)
  • A family member has been diagnosed with a genetic condition, and testing can determine if you carry the same variant
🆓
Cost: WGS is free on the NHS when referred by a hospital specialist. You do not need to pay for the test.

🩸 How the Test Works

The testing process is straightforward:

  1. Sample collection — A blood sample (or sometimes saliva) is taken. For cancer patients, a tumour sample already removed during treatment is used.
  2. Laboratory analysis — Your sample is sent to one of seven NHS Genomic Laboratory Hubs across England. Scientists extract your DNA and run it through a sequencing machine.
  3. Data analysis — Specialist bioinformaticians and clinical scientists analyse the sequence data, comparing it to a reference genome to identify variants.
  4. Results — Your results are sent to your clinical team, who will discuss them with you.
⏳
Timeline: Results typically take several weeks to months, depending on the complexity of the case. Your clinical team will let you know when to expect them.

👨‍👩‍👧 Trio Testing — Why Both Parents Are Asked to Provide Samples

For rare and inherited diseases, the most effective form of WGS is called trio testing. This means sequencing the genomes of the patient and both biological parents at the same time.

Why Is Trio Testing So Important?

Trio testing is considered the gold standard because:

  • Identifying new mutations: By comparing your genome to both parents, scientists can spot de novo variants — genetic changes that are new to you and were not inherited. These are a common cause of rare diseases.
  • Filtering inherited variants: Everyone carries thousands of benign genetic differences. Comparing with parents allows scientists to quickly filter these out and focus on the variants that matter.
  • Determining inheritance pattern: It helps establish whether a variant was inherited from the mother, the father, or both — which is critical for understanding the genetic condition and its recurrence risk.
  • Higher diagnostic yield: Trio testing significantly increases the likelihood of finding a diagnosis compared to testing the patient alone.

What If Both Parents Can't Be Tested?

Sometimes trio testing isn't possible — for example, if a parent is deceased, uncontactable, or unwilling. In these cases, alternatives include:

  • Duo testing: The patient is tested alongside one biological parent. This is less powerful than trio testing but still provides valuable information for filtering variants.
  • Singleton testing: Only the patient is tested. Analysis is still possible but is more complex, may take longer, and has a lower diagnostic rate.
  • Other family members: In some cases, siblings or other relatives may be asked to provide samples to support the analysis.
⚠️
Important: Non-Paternity and Trio Testing

Because trio testing compares the patient's genome to both parents, it will reveal if a stated biological parent is not the true biological parent (known as non-paternity or misattributed parentage). If discovered, this information must be disclosed because it directly affects the clinical interpretation of results. Read more about non-paternity disclosure →

📊
Clinical evidence: Studies show trio testing can achieve a diagnostic rate of around 30–50% for rare diseases, compared to approximately 20–25% for singleton analysis. This difference can mean the difference between getting a diagnosis or not.

📊 What Results Can Show

Your results may show one of several outcomes:

  • A clear diagnosis — A genetic change is found that is known to cause a health condition. This can help guide your treatment.
  • A variant of uncertain significance (VUS) — A genetic change is found but its meaning isn't fully understood yet. This may become clearer as scientific knowledge advances.
  • No significant result — No genetic change is found to explain your condition. This doesn't mean there isn't a genetic cause — it may be that current science hasn't identified it yet.
  • Unexpected or additional findings — Sometimes WGS reveals information unrelated to the original reason for testing. Read more about this.

👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 Impact on Your Family

Because your genome is inherited from your parents and shared (in part) with your blood relatives, your test results may also have implications for your family:

  • If a genetic condition is identified, other family members may also carry the variant and could be offered cascade testing
  • Your results could inform family planning decisions for you or your relatives
  • Your data may be compared to other patients' to help understand whether findings are significant
  • In some cases, WGS may reveal unexpected family relationships — read more about this
💬
Genetic Counselling

You may be referred to a genetic counsellor who can help you understand what your results mean for you and your family, and support you through the process. The Genetic Alliance UK also offers information and support.
